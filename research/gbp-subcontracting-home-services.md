# Can you win Google Maps for home services and subcontract all the work?

**The question:** What if you set up several Google Business Profiles (GBPs) for legit-looking landscaping or other home-service businesses in your city, out-optimized everyone on reviews, websites and local SEO, and subcontracted every job to a reliable group of contractors? Is it legit? Do people do it?

**Research date:** October 9, 2026
**Related files:**
- [creators-watchlist.md](creators-watchlist.md): creators we're studying (Zach P, Edward Sturm)
- [reel-transcripts-and-zach-weup.md](reel-transcripts-and-zach-weup.md): how to get reel transcripts

**Scope:** United States. The city and state weren't specified, so the state-specific parts are written for several states (see §5.3).
**Not legal advice.** Have a lawyer in your state check the licensing and worker-classification parts before you spend money.

> **How this was researched, and its limits.** The research ran as four parallel tracks: Google policy, US law, real-world examples, and ranking and money. They drew on roughly 250 web searches. Google's support pages, ftc.gov and most other primary sites couldn't be fetched directly from this environment because of a network proxy. Most quoted policy text therefore comes from search-engine extracts of the named pages, not from loading them. The two most important findings were spot-checked separately: the May 2026 FTC/DOJ case and the 2017 Google/UC San Diego study. Anything marked **[unverified]** should be confirmed before you rely on it. Reddit couldn't be reached, so the forum evidence comes from LawnSite, PlowSite and TractorByNet.

## 1. Short answer

**Yes, people do this, at every scale. But there are two versions of the idea, and only one is legit.**

| | **Version A: the idea as you described it** | **Version B: the legit version** |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Several GBPs for brands that "look legit." No crews; every job passed to a sub. | **One real company per real location.** You sign the customer contract, carry the license and insurance, and warranty the work. Subs supply the labor. |
| Google | Breaks **three separate written rules**: "lead generation agents or companies" can't have profiles; one profile per business (no separate profiles per service); no virtual or fake addresses. | Defensible, as long as you have real in-person contact with customers (estimates, walkthroughs, QC) under your own brand. Google has **not** explicitly blessed 100%-subcontracted businesses, so some risk remains. |
| Law | Matches the facts of a **live FTC/DOJ/Illinois/Minnesota lawsuit filed May 2026** against a home-services "middleman, or services broker" (§4.1). Fake reviews carry FTC penalties of **up to $53,088 per violation**. | Legal, if you're licensed where your state requires it and you classify and insure subs correctly. |
| Durability | One competitor report, failed video verification, or an account-level restriction can wipe out every profile at once. Reviews and rankings disappear with them. | It builds an asset you can sell. Businesses like this are listed for sale as "100% subcontracted" companies. |
| Economics | Looks like a cheat code. In practice you pay for the lead, the sub, and the risk. | Works for **project work** (hardscape, installs, irrigation, tree, fencing). Struggles on **weekly mowing**, where margins are thin and subs take your clients (§7). |

**The verdict:**
- Your marketing insight is real. Many small contractors neglect their GBP. Reviews, the right category and the name drive Map Pack rankings, and good operators do win this way.
- The **"multiple GBPs that look legit"** part is the part that gets people sued or wiped out. It isn't a gray area. Google has sued people over exactly this, explicitly calling out "rank and rent" (2023). The FTC and two state attorneys general are suing a company over a version of it right now (2026).
- The legit version is a **marketing-led general contractor**. It's a real business model, but it's harder and lower-margin than the fantasy version. Section 9 compares five ways to build it.

### 2.2 Individual operators

**"Rank and rent" (build a lead-gen site or profile, then rent it to a contractor).** This is the closest individual-scale match to your idea.
- **Ippei Kanehara** ([ippei.com](https://ippei.com/)) says sites rent for **$500–$3,000+ per month** and take 3–6 months to start producing leads. His own example is a tree-care site earning **$2,000 a month for 8+ years** from 30–50 leads a month. He sells a coaching program for about $3K–$5K.
- **Nick Wood (Digital Landlords / Flat Fee Mastery)** uses a "rent first, rank later" model: run Google Ads to generate leads straight away and charge contractors flat monthly fees. He claims a "7-figure" lead-gen business.
- **Dan Klein** and **Joshua T. Osborne (Digital Leasing)** are the other well-known teachers. One reviewer calls some of Osborne's claims "highly unlikely."
- **Correction:** "Lead Gen Jay" (Jay Feldman) teaches B2B cold email, not rank-and-rent.
- **Survivorship bias:** almost every income claim in this niche comes from people selling $3K–$10K courses. There's no independent data on how students actually do.
- **Important:** classic rank-and-rent ranks **websites** organically and with ads. Building fake **GBPs** is the version Google sued over (§4.2). Your plan depends on GBPs, so it carries **more** risk than the course version.

**Landscapers who subcontract** (LawnSite and Lawn & Landscape trade press):
- Owners who sub out mowing keep the customer relationship and billing. They add a 10–15% markup and require subs to carry their own insurance ([LawnSite thread](https://www.lawnsite.com/threads/what-do-i-need-to-do-to-subcontract-my-mowing.451564/); [lawn service brokers thread](https://www.lawnsite.com/threads/lawn-service-brokers.228360/)).
- One poster: "there isn't enough margin in subbing out residential mowing." Another reports a "decent markup" when subs get the day rate they want.
- A 2015 Lawn & Landscape roundtable concluded the **"cons outweigh the pros."** Owners described losing control of quality ("you didn't train them"), subs not showing up "because it's not their job," and **being "burned" by subs who went around them to the customer** ([Lawn & Landscape](https://www.lawnandlandscape.com/news/ll081915-subcontracting-pros-cons)).
- One residential owner says his two-man sub crews make **$400–$500 a day**. A commenter estimates a crew needs about **$1,000 a day** to cover its true costs ([TractorByNet](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/goto/post?id=4243944)). If the sub is underpaid, you get no-shows.

**Proof the model is bought and sold** (seller claims, unverified):
- A San Diego cleaning company where independent contractors do all the work, listed with a **30–35% net margin** ([BizQuest](https://www.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/subcontractor-model-cleaning-business-w-80k-revenue-and-30-margins/BW2454306)).
- "Turnkey Home Services Operation | No Equipment, No Crews, 100% Sub," projecting a **15% margin** ([BizQuest](https://images.bizquest.com/start-up-business/turnkey-home-services-operation-no-equipment-no-crews-100-sub/BW2398105)).
- A subcontractor-model roofing company ([BizQuest](https://images.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/home-based-roofing-business-subcontractor-model-and-strong-margins/BW2435221)).
- A search-fund forum calls the 100%-subcontracted model a "managed marketplace" that trades control over quality and scheduling for low overhead ([SearchFunder](https://searchfunder.com/post/thoughts-on-subcontractor-only-service-businesses-eg-lawntree-care)).

### 2.3 What the examples teach
1. **The survivors are real businesses.** LawnStarter, Lessen, ServiceChannel and Service Direct are all open about what they are. None pretends to be a crew of local mowers.
2. **Recurring residential mowing is a graveyard for middlemen.** Lawn Love was absorbed, TaskEasy was sold to a software company, and practitioners keep repeating "not enough margin."
3. **Being the contracting party means owning the liability.** Handy ($6M misclassification) and Home Depot ($20.75M for its contractors' lead-paint violations) both paid for it.
4. **Paying subs badly destroys the brand.** Low or slow pay leads to no-shows and bad work, and the 1-star reviews land on **your** profile.

## 3. Google's rules: why "several GBPs" doesn't work

### 3.1 What the guidelines say
These come from Google's [Guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), [Business eligibility and ownership guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13763036), [service-area business help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481) and [duplicates help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/12756178). The text is from search extracts.

- **In-person contact is required.** "To qualify for a Business Profile on Google, a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours."
- **Lead generation is explicitly ineligible.** The examples of ineligible businesses include **"Lead generation agents or companies."** Google doesn't define the term.
- **One profile per business.** "There should only be one profile per business…" and "Do not create more than one page for each location of your business, either in a single account or multiple accounts."
- **No separate profiles per service.** If you offer different services, "you shouldn't create separate Business Profiles for each service." Multiple profiles for the same business "may mislead customers and are against Google's policies." So "Lawn Pros of X" plus "X Hardscaping" plus "X Tree Service," all run by you with the same subs, is the textbook violation.
- **Multiple locations need real staff.** "If you have different locations for your service business, with separate service areas and separate staff at each location, you're allowed one profile for each location."
- **No virtual offices or mailboxes.** "If your business rents a physical mailing address but doesn't operate out of that location, also known as a virtual office, that location isn't eligible." P.O. boxes and remote mailboxes aren't allowed. A co-working space qualifies only with signage, customers received during business hours, **and your own staff** there.
- **The name must be your real-world name.** It must match your signage and branding. Keyword stuffing isn't allowed.
- **Service-area businesses** may hide their address, set up to 20 service areas, and should cover no more than about 2 hours' drive from base.

### 3.2 Verification: hard to fake honestly
Video verification is now the default for many new profiles ([Google help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/14271705)). It requires one continuous, unedited clip showing:
1. **Location:** street signs or the building.
2. **Proof the business exists:** professional tools or equipment, a **branded vehicle**, branded apparel, business cards.
3. **Proof of management:** you "performing a service," "unlocking a van that has your business branding," or business documents.

A subcontract-only operator with no tools, truck or job sites under each brand **can't show this honestly for one brand, let alone several.** Changing the name, address or category can trigger re-verification.

### 3.3 Enforcement scale
- **Google's numbers.**
  - 2024: blocked or removed **240M+ policy-violating reviews and 12M+ fake profiles**, and removed more than 10,000 locksmith-impersonation listings ([SERoundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-maps-spam-fighting-2024-39186.html)).
  - 2025, published April 2026: **292M+ reviews and 13M+ fake profiles**, and about 782K accounts restricted ([Transparency Report](https://transparencyreport.google.com/maps-content/enforcement); [SERoundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-maps-spam-fighting-2025-41176.html)).
- **Account-level restrictions.** If Google restricts your *account*, "the Business Profiles you manage are suspended and you can't create or claim other profiles" ([Google help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4569145)). **For a multi-profile operator, one strike takes down everything.**
- **Competitors can report you.** Anyone can file the [Business Redressal Complaint Form](https://support.google.com/business/contact/business_redressal_form), attaching evidence and batching reports. Home services is among the most heavily policed categories. Google Product Experts like Jason Brown ([reviewfraud.org](https://searchlabdigital.com/blog/suds-and-search-32-jason-brown-gmb-spam-fighter-and-google-gold-product-expert)) hunt lead-gen networks as a hobby. **Your competitors are the enforcement mechanism.**
- **Review penalties.** Profiles caught with fake reviews can have reviews removed and new reviews blocked, and can show a public warning such as "Suspicious high-rated reviews were recently removed from this place." Google expanded these warnings to the US in April 2025 ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/04/07/google-maps-fake-reviews/)).
- **2026 review-policy tightening.** In April 2026 Google banned asking staff to collect a set number of reviews and asking reviewers to mention specific content such as a technician's name ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-tightens-maps-review-policy-staff-names-and-quotas-now-banned/)). Subcontractors and their families count as conflicts of interest, so they can't review you.

### 3.4 Local Services Ads (the "Google Verified" ads at the top) block subcontracting
From the [Local Services platform policies](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6245891):
- Providers "may not sell the leads." Leads must be fulfilled "by the vetted and/or background-checked field technicians of your company."
- "If you subcontract to another service provider your ad may be permanently removed."
- "Once a commitment is made, the business that advertised must be the one to fulfill that commitment."
- "Advertising on behalf of call-centers that re-route requests to other businesses not listed in your ads is not allowed."
- Where background checks apply, they cover "the business, the business owner, and their field worker roster (employees, contractors, **including subcontractors**…)."

**What this means:** LSAs only work for you if every sub's field workers pass Google's background check **on your account**. That's possible in principle with a small, stable group of subs, but not with whoever is free that day. Two recent changes: the Guaranteed, Screened and License Verified badges were merged into one **"Google Verified"** badge in October 2025, and LSAs are moving into a Performance Max pay-per-lead campaign type from August 2026 into 2027 ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-local-services-ads-verified-badge-461001); [SERoundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-local-services-ads-transition-google-ads-41717.html)).

### 3.5 The gray zone: a general contractor who subcontracts 100%
**I found no Google statement on this either way.** The practical line practitioners draw is:
- **Probably OK:** the customer contracts with **you**. You're responsible for the outcome. You meet customers in person for estimates, site visits and final walkthroughs, under your own real brand, from a real base.
- **Lead generation (ineligible):** you take the call and **hand it off**, and the customer effectively deals with someone else.

General contractors who sub out every trade have GBPs all the time. The defensible version looks like that. The weak points are verification (you need *your* branded vehicle and presence) and a competitor report that frames you as lead gen.

## 4. Cautionary tales: Version A in court

### 4.1 United States v. B.E.S.T. GDR, LLC d/b/a "Premium Home Service" (filed May 11, 2026)
This is the closest real-world match to your idea. **These are allegations; nothing has been proven.**
- **Who sued:** the DOJ on behalf of the FTC, together with the Illinois AG, in the Northern District of Illinois (case 1:26-cv-05415). The **Minnesota AG** filed a parallel suit the same day.
- **What's alleged:**
  - **15,000+ Google profiles** for fictitious "local" home-repair companies, using addresses that often belonged to unrelated businesses.
  - Local phone numbers that routed to call centers, including in the Philippines.
  - Fake five-star reviews posted to drown out real one-star reviews.
  - Staff photos taken from unrelated websites.
  - **No technicians of its own:** jobs were passed to third-party contractors, some allegedly unlicensed or unqualified.
- **Minnesota's key line:** "Unbeknownst to consumers, Defendants are a **middleman, or services broker**." Minnesota also sued under its **Commercial Assumed Names Act** (unregistered brand names).
- **Legal claims:** FTC Act §5 (deception), the FTC Consumer Reviews Rule, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, Illinois consumer fraud law, and Minnesota consumer protection law.
- **The FTC's consumer-protection director:** "Premium Home Service's use of fake business profiles and reviews violates federal and state laws, harming consumers and businesses."
- **Status:** the defendants moved to dismiss on July 17, 2026, and discovery was stayed. Secondhand reports put the network at 7,600+ phone numbers and about $79M in profit **[unverified]**.
- **Sources:** [DOJ press release](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-files-complaint-against-best-gdr-llc-doing-business-premium-home-service) · [FTC case page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/best-gdr-llc-et-al-united-states-state-illinois-v) · [Complaint PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/premium_home_service_-_filed_complaint.pdf) · [Minnesota AG](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2026/05/11_Premium-Home-Services.asp) · [Covington analysis](https://www.insideprivacy.com/united-states/federal-trade-commission/ftc-and-doj-continue-focus-on-consumer-reviews-rule-with-complaint-against-premium-home-service/)

**Takeaway:** your plan at 10 profiles instead of 15,000, and without the call centers, is still the same *theory of liability*: invented local identities, an undisclosed broker, subcontracted labor, and seeded reviews. Scale changes how likely you are to be noticed, not whether it's legal.

### 4.2 Google v. Ethan QiQi Hu / GMBEye / Rafadigital (filed June 16, 2023, N.D. Cal.)
- Google's complaint **explicitly targets "rank and rent."**
- **Alleged:** **350+ fake profiles** and **14,000+ fake reviews**, fake addresses, template websites, local-area-code VoIP numbers, and fraudulent verification. The profiles, or the leads from them, were sold to real businesses.
- The defendant was a member of the Facebook group **"Rank and Rent – GMB Strategy & Domination."**
- **Claims:** Lanham Act false advertising, breach of contract, and California unfair competition law.
- **Sources:** [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-lawsuit-rank-and-rent/489666/) · [SERoundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-sues-fake-business-profiles-reviews-35569.html)

### 4.3 Google v. Yaniv Asayag (filed March 2025, N.D. Cal. 5:25-cv-02642)
- About **10,000 fake or hijacked listings** for locksmiths, HVAC and towing.
- Callers were routed to unrelated providers, and their details sold as leads.
- **Sources:** [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/google-maps-fake-listings-lawsuit-scams/) · [Complaint](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.446643/gov.uscourts.cand.446643.1.0.pdf)

### 4.4 Journalism and research
- **Wall Street Journal, June 2019:** about **11 million** falsely listed businesses on Google Maps on any given day, concentrated in contractors, electricians, towing, movers and lawyers. In one NYC plumber search, **13 of the top 20 results had fake addresses** ([Engadget summary](https://www.engadget.com/2019-06-20-wsj-google-maps-is-filled-with-fake-business-listings.html)).
- **New York Times, January 2016:** fake "local" locksmith listings routed to call centers. Phone quotes of $35–$90, then subcontractors who arrived and charged 3–4× that ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/googles-fake-locksmith-problem-once-again-hits-the-new-york-times-241473)).
- **Google and UC San Diego study, 2017:** **40% of fake Maps listings were "on-call contractors"** such as plumbers and locksmiths, and 11% of locksmith search results were fraudulent. Scammers used fake locations "to make it look like their business is in close proximity to the user." Google cut such listings by 70% from their peak ([UC San Diego](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/pinning_down_abuse_on_google_maps); [Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2017/04/new-research-keeping-fake-listings-off.html)).

**Why this matters to you:** fake profiles for home services are among the most-studied, most-reported and most-litigated forms of spam on Google Maps. You'd be entering the exact category Google, regulators and competitors watch most closely.

## 5. The law, beyond Google's rules

### 5.1 FTC Consumer Reviews Rule (16 CFR Part 465, effective October 21, 2024)
[Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/22/2024-18519/trade-regulation-rule-on-the-use-of-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials)

What it bans:
- **Fake reviews** from people who don't exist or didn't use the service. Buying them and knowingly spreading them are also covered.
- **Incentives conditioned on a positive review.** A neutral "leave a review and get 10% off, good or bad" is allowed under the FTC rule. **Google bans all incentives anyway.**
- **Undisclosed insider reviews** from officers, managers, employees or agents.
- **Company-controlled "independent" review sites.** A "Best Landscapers in [City]" site that ranks your own brands is exactly this.
- **Review suppression** through threats, or by showing only the good reviews while implying they're all of them.
- **Fake social signals** such as bought followers or views.

Penalties:
- **Up to $53,088 per violation.** The 2026 inflation adjustment was cancelled, so the 2025 figure still applies ([FTC](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-publishes-inflation-adjusted-civil-penalty-amounts-2025)).

Enforcement so far:
- **Warning letters to 10 companies, December 2025** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-warns-10-companies-about-possible-violations-agencys-new-consumer-review-rule)).
- **TruHeight settlement, 2026:** reported at about $750K **[order status unverified]**.
- The Premium Home Service case (§4.1).

### 5.2 FTC Act §5 and state consumer-protection laws
Misrepresenting **who does the work** or that you're a **local** company can be deceptive. That includes claims like "our technicians," "family-owned local crew," made-up offices, or brands presented as independent competitors. That's the core theory of the Premium Home Service and Minnesota complaints. New York's "Operation Clean Turf" (2013) fined 19 companies about $350K over fake reviews under state law ([WNYC](https://wnyc.org/story/new-york-cracks-down-fake-online-reviews/)).

**Multiple brands are not illegal in themselves.** They become deceptive when the brands:
- claim to be independent or locally owned;
- invent separate offices;
- review each other or rank each other on "independent" sites;
- hand out fake "competing" quotes.

**Safe approach:** register each brand as a DBA, give each a real address, and add "a [Parent Co.] company" disclosures.

### 5.3 Contractor licensing: subcontracting usually doesn't get you out of it
**The core principle:** most states that license this work define "contractor" to include the person who **arranges** it, not only the person holding the shovel.
- **California** (B&P §7026): a contractor includes anyone who does the work "himself or herself **or by or through others**," or offers or bids to.
  - Unlicensed contracting means you **can't sue for payment** (§7031(a)), and the customer can sue to get back **everything they paid** (§7031(b)), with no offset for materials or labor.
  - **Even a licensed GC was barred from collecting for an unlicensed sub's work** in *Kim v. TWA Construction* (2022).
  - The state licensing board (CSLB) says referral services are legal "but within limits": they can't negotiate contracts or offer to do the project themselves ([CSLB bulletin](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Media_Room/Industry_Bulletins/2018/July_20.aspx)).
- **Arizona** (A.R.S. §32-1101): covers anyone who "purports to have the capacity to undertake" covered work.
- **New Jersey** (N.J.S.A. 56:8-137): covers anyone in the business of "making **or selling**" home improvements.

**Landscaping by state:**

| State | Statewide landscaping license? | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **California** | **Yes**, CSLB **C-27** ([CSLB](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/About_us/Library/Licensing_Classifications/Licensing_Classifications_Detail.aspx?Class=C27)) | Required for jobs of **$1,000+** (labor plus materials; raised from $500 by AB 2622, effective 2025) or any job needing a permit. C-27 expressly covers "subcontracting" landscape systems. Routine mowing, weeding and trimming generally needs no license; irrigation, lighting, walls, patios and drainage do. Advertising without a license is a misdemeanor (§7027.1). Pesticides need separate DPR licensing. |
| **Arizona** | **Yes**, ROC C-21 / R-21 / CR-21 (hardscape and irrigation) | Handyman exemption only under $1,000 with no permit. Maintenance-only gardeners are exempt. |
| **Nevada** | **Yes**, NSCB **C-10** | Exemption for repair or maintenance work under $1,000. |
| **Texas** | **No** | **Irrigation needs a TCEQ Landscape Irrigator license.** Applying pesticides for hire needs a TDA license. **Landscaping and lawn care are subject to sales tax** as "real property services" (34 TAC §3.356). |
| **Florida** | **No** | Local Business Tax Receipt; some counties license. **Commercial fertilizer application requires certification** (F.S. 482.1562). Pesticides need FDACS certification. |
| **New York** | No statewide license | **NYC requires a Home Improvement Contractor license that expressly covers residential landscaping.** Some counties (e.g., Nassau, Suffolk, Westchester) license locally **[unverified]**. An unlicensed contractor **can't collect** (*Hamptons Landscaping v. Sherman*, 2017). |
| **New Jersey** | **Yes**, Home Improvement Contractor registration (covers residential landscaping) | Sellers and brokers count as contractors. |
| **Connecticut** | Home Improvement Contractor registration (landscaping listed in the definition) | Tree work needs a DEEP arborist license. Confirm the current definition. |
| **Maryland** | **Yes**, MHIC (landscape contractors licensed) | Mowing reportedly exempt. Tree work needs a DNR tree expert license. |
| **Washington** | L&I contractor registration for landscape *construction* | Lawn care and pruning reportedly exempt. $15K bond. |
| **Massachusetts** | Home Improvement Contractor registration for owner-occupied 1–4 unit homes | **Coverage of landscaping unverified.** |

**Almost everywhere:** applying pesticides or weed-and-feed for hire needs a state applicator license. Tree work, irrigation and backflow testing are often licensed separately. **A sub's license doesn't cover *your* obligation to be licensed when you advertise and sign the contract.**

### 5.4 Worker classification
- **The trap in ABC-test states** (California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, partly Illinois and Maryland): if your business is landscaping, individual landscapers doing your jobs are inside your "usual course of business." That fails prong B and makes them **employees**. This is exactly how Handy ended up paying $6M.
- **California's construction-subcontractor exemption** (Labor Code §2781) lets you use the older, more lenient test only if the sub holds a **CSLB license**, has a written contract, a business location, its own insurance, and authority to hire its own help.
  - **Mowing and maintenance subs usually aren't CSLB-licensed, so they can't use this exemption.**
  - The business-to-business exemption (§2776) is a poor fit because the sub would be serving *your customers*.
- **New Jersey's** new ABC rules took effect **October 1, 2026**.
- **The lower-risk structure:**
  - Subcontract only with **real businesses**: their own entity, employees, license, insurance, equipment and other clients.
  - Use written, job-based subcontracts and pay per job.
  - Specify results, not methods.
  - Don't require exclusivity.
  - Check their license and insurance every season.
- **The tension:** heavy brand control (uniforms, scripts, your truck wraps) increases classification risk.

### 5.5 You carry the liability as prime contractor
- **To the customer:** you signed the contract, so the defects are yours regardless of which sub did the work. Hiring an unlicensed or uninsured sub can also expose you to negligent-selection claims.
- **Workers' comp ("statutory employer"):** if an uninsured sub's worker gets hurt, **you can be on the hook.** Examples: Florida §440.10, New York WCL §56, Massachusetts c.152 §18, and California §2750.5 (an unlicensed sub's workers are presumed to be *your* employees).
- **Insurance:**
  - **You:** general liability with completed operations, an "if any" workers' comp policy, and hired and non-owned auto.
  - **From subs:** require the actual **additional-insured endorsements** (CG 20 10 and CG 20 37, or blanket equivalents), not just a certificate, plus a waiver of subrogation.
  - Indemnity clauses are limited by anti-indemnity laws in about 45 states.
- **Mechanics' liens:** if you don't pay a sub, **the sub can lien your customer's house**, even if the customer paid you. Use conditional and unconditional lien waivers with each payment.
- **Home-improvement contract rules:** California B&P §7159 caps the down payment at 10% or $1,000, whichever is less, and requires set contract contents. The FTC Cooling-Off Rule gives a 3-day right to cancel sales made at the customer's home. NY, NJ, CT, MA and MD have similar rules **[mostly unverified details]**.

### 5.6 Other legal issues
- **Subs stealing your customers:**
  - California voids most non-competes and many non-solicits (B&P §16600). Narrow **business-to-business** non-circumvention clauses may survive.
  - Other states generally enforce reasonable non-solicits.
  - **The real protection is operational:** own the contract, billing, CRM and customer communication.
- **Texting and calling leads (the TCPA, the federal law on marketing calls and texts):** you need written consent for automated marketing texts and calls. Damages are $500–$1,500 per violation. Stick to responding to inbound inquiries and capture consent on your forms.
- **DBA registration** for every brand name. The Minnesota AG sued under its assumed-names law.

## 6. How Google Maps ranking works (and why the multi-GBP trick is tempting)

Google says local results are "based primarily on **relevance, distance, and prominence**," and that there's "no way to request or pay for a better local ranking" ([Google](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)).

**Top Map Pack factors** (Whitespark Local Search Ranking Factors, "2026" edition, published November 2025; an expert survey, via secondary summaries [[Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/)]):
1. Primary GBP category
2. **Proximity of the address to the searcher**
3. **Keywords in the business name**
4. Physical address in the city being searched
5. Open at the time of the search

**Proximity is why spammers make multiple profiles.**
- Since Google's **"Vicinity" update (December 2021)**, Map Pack results have become much more "zoomed in" ([Sterling Sky](https://www.sterlingsky.ca/vicinity-algorithm-update/)).
- Whitespark says service-area businesses "can only rank in a small radius around the address associated with their GBP, regardless of whether the address is shown," and that the service-areas field **doesn't affect ranking** ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/blog/do-service-areas-impact-rankings-in-2024/)).
- So each verified address creates a new ranking "bubble." That's the whole reason fake-address networks exist, and why the 2017 study found scammers faking proximity.

**Keyword in the name.**
- A September 2026 Whitespark study of 1.4M listings found home-service listings with a keyword in the name reached the top 3 about **2.1× as often** as those without ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/blog/study-how-keywords-in-business-names-currently-impact-google-local-pack-rank-and-ai-search/)). It's a correlation only.
- **The legit version:** make the keyword part of your *actual* legal or DBA brand from day one (e.g., "[Neighborhood] Lawn & Landscape"), on your truck, signage and invoices. Stuffing keywords into a profile name that doesn't match your real brand is a violation.

**Reviews.**
- The median business ranking in the 3-pack has **47 reviews**, with ratings clustered at **4.5–4.9 stars** (Local Falcon, 50.4M results, January 2026 [[Local Falcon](https://www.localfalcon.com/blog/whitepaper-google-reviews-ratings--location-what-50-million-search-results-reveal-about-ranking-in-the-local-3pack)]). Competitive metros run much higher. One practitioner estimate is 200–500+ in big-metro trades **[vendor]**.
- Review **volume and recency** matter more for ranking than star rating.
- **What customers do** (BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, via summaries [[PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/)]):
  - **47%** won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews.
  - **68%** require 4.0+ stars, and **31%** require 4.5+.
  - **74%** look for reviews from the last 3 months.
  - **88%** would use a business that responds to all reviews.

**Can you "beat the hell out of" competitors?**
- **Partly.** BrightLocal (2025) found only **35% of small businesses have a Google Business Profile at all** ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/smb-marketing-2025/)), so plenty of contractors neglect it.
- **But the three already in the Map Pack are, by definition, the ones who don't neglect it.**
- One legit profile can realistically dominate **a few miles around your real address**. Covering a whole metro takes paid channels or genuinely staffed extra locations.
- **My estimate:** at a realistic 5–15 new reviews a month from real customers, reaching 50–150 reviews takes roughly 6–18 months.

**The AI and ads squeeze.**
- AI Overviews appear on about 92% of informational local queries but only about 15% of "trade + town" searches, where the Map Pack still shows more than 90% of the time (Whitespark, May 2025, secondhand).
- But **45% of consumers now use AI tools for local recommendations** (BrightLocal 2026), and Google use for finding local businesses fell from 83% to 71%. Reviews across Google, Yelp, Nextdoor and similar sites feed those AI answers.
- LSAs and ads sit above the Map Pack on most home-service searches. In a 100-keyword study, LSAs appeared on 78 keywords, though in only about 28% of all searches ([WebFX](https://www.webfx.com/blog/home-services/local-services-ads-vs-google-ads-vs-local-pack-study/)).

### 7.2 Worked unit economics (my calculations; the assumptions drive everything)

**The core tension:** a typical landscaper nets 10–17%. Your 15–25% cut is **bigger than the sub's whole profit.** It only works if either:
- you charge the customer a premium (better service, communication, warranty); or
- you replace the sub's own marketing costs and fill their idle days.

Their alternative, about $10–$75 for a shared lead, sets the most they'll tolerate.

| Scenario | Revenue to you | Customer acquisition cost | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weekly mowing:** $56 × 28 visits = ~$1,570 a season; 20% cut | ~$314 a season | $104–$118 per lead at an assumed 40% close = **~$260–$295** | **About break-even in year 1.** Profit depends on multi-year retention, which is exactly what a sub who sees the customer every week can take from you. **Worst fit.** |
| **Paver patio:** $3,800; 20% markup on a ~$3,167 sub price | ~$633 | $118 per lead at an assumed 20% close = **~$590**, plus your time on estimate visits | **Thin.** |
| **Design-build:** $15,000; 20% markup on cost | ~$2,500 | ~$600–$900 | **Healthy.** This is where the model works. |
| **Tree removal:** $750; 20% | ~$150 | Cheap if LSA works, but LSA rules fight subcontracting | **Marginal.** Liability is high (inherently dangerous work). |

**Which services fit a subcontract model:**
- **Best:** high-ticket, one-off projects such as hardscape and patios, design-build installs, irrigation, sod, retaining walls, fencing and outdoor lighting. Aspire lists hardscape, irrigation and tree as the usual subcontracted specialties ([Aspire](https://www.youraspire.com/blog/how-to-subcontract-landscaping-work)).
- **Plausible add-ons:** seasonal or one-off work like holiday lighting, gutter cleaning and pressure washing. These weren't researched in depth.
- **Worst:** recurring residential mowing, where dollars are thin and the risk of losing the customer to the sub is highest.
- **Seasonality:** demand peaks in April–May and bottoms out in October–November, with a fall-cleanup bump in September–October.

### 7.3 Running it
- **Vetting subs:** license; general liability, workers' comp and auto certificates re-checked each year; additional-insured endorsements; references; past work; a small trial job first ([NALP](https://blog.landscapeprofessionals.org/business-smarts-effectively-partnering-with-subcontractors/)).
- **Quality control:** before and after photos on every job, your own walkthrough on projects, and termination-for-performance clauses. One firm pays someone to inspect subs on site against the work order ([Lawn & Landscape](https://www.lawnandlandscape.com/article/filling-the-gaps--subcontracting)).
- **Preventing poaching:** own all customer communication and billing. Subs never invoice customers. Use a narrow non-circumvention clause with business subs. Brickman reportedly used a 24-month customer non-solicit ([Turf Magazine](https://turfmagazine.com/would-you-agree-to-this-contracts-terms/)).
- **Software:** **Workiz** has a real subcontractor mode, where subs see only their jobs, upload photos and close jobs ([Workiz](https://help.workiz.com/hc/en-us/articles/18054328125329-Allowing-subcontractors-to-collect-payments-and-close-jobs)). Jobber needs a paid seat for each sub. Aspire has a separate markup category for subcontractor costs.
- **Pay subs well and fast.** Every cautionary example (SMS Assist, LawnStarter pros) shows that squeezing subs produces no-shows and bad work, and the reviews land on you.

## 9. If you want to do this: five legit ways to build it

**Option 1: a marketing-led general contractor (the legit version of your idea).**
- One brand with the keyword in its *real* name (DBA filed); licensed where your state requires; general liability and an "if any" workers' comp policy; one GBP verified at your **real** base. A home-based service-area business with a hidden address is fine.
- **You personally** do estimates, site visits and final walkthroughs in a **branded vehicle** with branded apparel. That's genuine in-person contact and it makes video verification honest.
- Focus on **project work**. Use 3–6 licensed, insured subcontracting *companies* under written subcontracts, with lien waivers and photo QC.
- Add Google Ads, organic suburb pages, Nextdoor, direct mail near jobs (response near existing customers was reported at about 4.2% versus 0.8% elsewhere [anecdotal]), and relationships with property managers.
- Expand into a new area only by opening a **real staffed location**.
- *Pros:* builds a sellable asset. *Cons:* the most legal setup work, and you own the liability.

**Option 2: a marketing partnership with one good contractor.**
- You bring the marketing, website and review system. They bring crews, license and insurance, **under their real brand and GBP**.
- Revenue share, or a fee per closed job.
- *Pros:* almost no licensing, classification or liability exposure for you. *Cons:* you don't own the brand. Get a written agreement covering the revenue share and what happens if you part ways.

**Option 3: a marketing agency for contractors.**
- Run real contractors' GBPs, review systems and ads for a retainer or a fee per lead.
- This turns the same "most landscapers are bad at Google" insight into money with **zero** policy risk.
- *Pros:* clean, scalable, no crews. *Cons:* service-business churn, and income capped by client count.

**Option 4: honest website-only lead gen (rank-and-rent without fake GBPs).**
- Build city- and service-specific websites. Rank them organically and run ads. Sell the calls to contractors, or to networks like Service Direct that buy calls from publishers.
- Rules: no fake reviews, no fake addresses, no "our crews" claims.
- California-type rules limit what a referral service can do: no negotiating contracts and no offering to do the work.
- *Pros:* low cost. *Cons:* no Map Pack presence, which is the most valuable real estate, plus the course-seller survivorship bias in §2.2.

**Option 5: buy a small landscaping company.**
- It comes with existing reviews, profile, license and crews. Then you add your marketing on top.
- *Pros:* real operations from day one, plus an aged profile with reviews. *Cons:* needs capital, and you inherit whatever problems the business has.

**My recommendation:**
- **Start with Option 2 or 3** to prove you can generate leads cheaply in your city at low risk.
- **Graduate to Option 1** once you know your cost per lead and close rates, and have 3–5 subcontracting companies you trust.
- **Skip the multiple-profile strategy entirely.** The upside is a few extra Map Pack spots for a while. The downside is losing every profile at once, plus personal exposure under FTC and state law, as the 2026 cases show.

## 10. What to check for your city before spending money
1. **Licensing:** your state contractor board's rules for landscaping, hardscape, irrigation, tree and pesticide work, plus city or county licensing, as a business that **contracts but subcontracts the labor**. Especially check whether "arranging" or "selling" the work triggers a license.
2. **Classification:** whether your state uses an ABC test, and whether your planned subs (licensed businesses or individuals) would qualify as independent.
3. **Sales tax:** whether landscaping services are taxable in your state (they are in Texas, for example).
4. **Insurance:** quotes for general liability with completed operations, an "if any" workers' comp policy, and hired and non-owned auto. Draft a subcontractor agreement with additional-insured, indemnity, non-circumvention and lien-waiver terms. Aspire has a [template](https://youraspire.com/templates/landscaping/subcontractor-agreement) as a starting point.
5. **Competition:** search "landscaping near me," "landscaper [city]" and "patio installer [city]" from 5–10 points across your city. Record the top-3 profiles' review counts, ratings and categories. That gives your real review target.
6. **Lead costs:** a small Google Ads test ($500–$1,000) in your target ZIP codes to measure your actual cost per lead before trusting the benchmarks above.
7. **A one-hour consult with a local construction or business lawyer** on the contract structure and licensing. It costs far less than the §7031(b) "refund everything" risk.
